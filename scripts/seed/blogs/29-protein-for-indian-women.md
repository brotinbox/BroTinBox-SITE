> **Quick answer:** ICMR-NIN sets the basic protein requirement for adults at **0.83 g per kg of body weight** per day. That is the minimum for a mostly inactive adult. If you exercise, are losing weight or want to keep muscle as you age, **1.2–1.6 g per kg** is a practical target. For a 60 kg woman, that is about **70–95 g a day**, spread across 3–4 meals. Eating more protein will not make you "bulky".

Many Indian women eat far less protein than they need. A typical day of idli or dosa, rice with sambar, a small vegetable side, chapati and tea can deliver as little as 35–45 g of protein. It tastes good, but it often leaves you hungry between meals, tired in the afternoon and losing muscle if you diet.

This guide covers how much protein you need, the myths that hold women back, and how to reach your number with everyday Indian food.

# How much protein do you need?

Start with your body weight in kilograms and pick the line that matches you best:

- **Mostly sitting, general health:** 0.83–1.0 g per kg
- **Walking, yoga or light exercise most days:** 1.0–1.2 g per kg
- **Losing weight:** 1.2–1.6 g per kg, to protect your muscle while you eat less
- **Strength training 3 or more days a week:** 1.4–1.8 g per kg
- **Over 50:** at least 1.0–1.2 g per kg, because muscle loss speeds up with age

## Worked examples

- **55 kg, office job, little exercise:** 55 × 0.9 = **about 50 g per day**
- **60 kg, trying to lose weight, walking daily:** 60 × 1.3 = **about 80 g per day**
- **65 kg, lifting weights 4 days a week:** 65 × 1.6 = **about 105 g per day**

If you have a lot of weight to lose, calculate using your goal weight instead of your current weight. Our general [protein per day guide](/blog/how-much-protein-per-day) has more examples.

> If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, or have kidney disease, your needs are different. Follow the advice of your doctor or a registered dietitian.

# Four myths that hold women back

## Myth 1: "Protein will make me bulky"

Building large muscles takes years of heavy training, a calorie surplus and, in large part, high levels of testosterone. Most women have far lower testosterone levels than men. Eating enough protein, especially while lifting weights, gives you a firmer, stronger shape. It does not make you look like a bodybuilder.

## Myth 2: "Protein is only for gym people"

Protein keeps you full, protects your muscle when you lose weight, and helps maintain strength as you age. These matter whether or not you ever enter a gym.

## Myth 3: "Too much protein damages the kidneys"

In healthy adults, the protein ranges in this guide are considered safe. People with existing kidney disease do need to limit protein, which is why that group should follow medical advice.

## Myth 4: "Vegetarians cannot get enough protein"

It takes more planning, but it is very possible. Paneer, curd, soya, dals, chana, rajma, sprouts and milk can easily add up to 70–90 g a day. Our [protein comparison](/blog/chicken-eggs-paneer-soya-protein-comparison) shows which sources give you the most protein per calorie.

# Spread it across the day

Your body uses protein best when each meal has a meaningful amount, roughly **20–30 g per meal** for most women. A tiny portion at breakfast and lunch followed by a big dinner is less useful than an even spread.

Here is what 20–30 g looks like:

- **3 eggs**, or 2 eggs with a glass of milk
- **100 g paneer** (about 18 g), plus a cup of curd
- **150 g cooked chicken or fish** (about 35–45 g)
- **50 g dry soya chunks**, cooked (about 25 g)
- **A large bowl of dal plus a cup of curd** (about 15–20 g)

## A sample vegetarian day (about 75 g protein)

- **Breakfast:** 2 pesarattu with a cup of curd
- **Lunch:** rice or chapati with a large bowl of dal, a soya or paneer curry, and salad
- **Snack:** a handful of roasted chana or sundal
- **Dinner:** chapati with chana masala and a cup of curd

# Two nutrients to watch alongside protein

## Iron

Anaemia is very common among Indian women. In the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5), about **57% of women aged 15–49** were anaemic. ICMR-NIN's recommended intake for adult women is about **29 mg of iron a day**, which is hard to reach from food alone.

- Good sources: green leafy vegetables, dals, rajma, chana, kollu, dates, eggs, meat and fish.
- Eat iron-rich foods with **vitamin C** (lemon, amla, guava, tomato) to help absorption.
- Avoid tea or coffee with your main meals, because they reduce iron absorption.
- If you feel constantly tired, breathless or dizzy, ask your doctor for a blood test before taking supplements.

## Calcium

ICMR-NIN recommends about **1,000 mg of calcium a day** for adults. Milk, curd, paneer, ragi and sesame are good sources. Calcium matters more with age, because bone density drops after menopause. Strength training also helps protect your bones.

# Make it easy

Most women do not fall short of protein because they do not care. They fall short because they are cooking for the whole family, eating whatever is left, and putting their own plate last.

A few simple habits help:

- Add a **cup of curd** to lunch and dinner.
- Keep **boiled eggs or roasted chana** ready for snacks.
- Add **paneer or soya** to the family's vegetable curry.
- Cook a **larger batch of dal** and take a full bowl yourself.

# Frequently asked questions

**Can women take protein powder?**
Yes. A protein powder is simply food in powder form. It is useful if you struggle to reach your target, but whole foods should come first. Read our comparison of [protein powder vs real food](/blog/protein-powder-vs-real-food-india).

**Does protein help with PCOS?**
A diet with enough protein and fibre can help with fullness and steadier blood sugar, which many women with PCOS find useful. PCOS needs individual care, so work with your doctor on a plan.

**How much protein do I need after 50?**
Aim for at least 1.0–1.2 g per kg each day, spread across meals, along with strength training. This helps slow the muscle and bone loss that speed up after menopause.

**Will eating more protein help me lose weight?**
It helps indirectly. Protein keeps you full for longer and protects your muscle, which makes a calorie deficit easier to stick to. You still need to eat fewer calories than you burn to lose weight.
