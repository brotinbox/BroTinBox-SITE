> **Quick answer:** **100 g of cooked chicken breast has about 31 g of protein** and about 165 kcal. 100 g of raw chicken breast has about 22–23 g of protein (chicken loses water when cooked, so the same piece weighs less and the protein per 100 g goes up). Cooked skinless thigh has about 25 g of protein per 100 g, with more fat.

Chicken is the most popular protein source for gym-goers in India, and for good reason: it is high in protein, low in fat (if you choose the right cut) and easy to cook in a hundred ways.

But the numbers people quote online are confusing. Is it 22 g or 31 g? Raw or cooked? With bone or without? Here is the clear version.

# Protein in chicken per 100 g

These are average values for chicken **without skin and without bone**.

- **Chicken breast, raw:** 22–23 g protein, about 110–120 kcal
- **Chicken breast, cooked (grilled or boiled):** about 31 g protein, about 165 kcal
- **Chicken thigh, raw:** about 19–20 g protein, about 120–140 kcal
- **Chicken thigh, cooked:** about 25 g protein, about 180–210 kcal
- **Drumstick meat, cooked:** about 24 g protein, about 170–190 kcal
- **Chicken liver, cooked:** about 24 g protein, about 165 kcal

With the **skin on**, protein per 100 g drops slightly and fat goes up a lot. Chicken skin is mostly fat.

# Raw vs cooked: why the numbers differ

This is the number one source of confusion.

When you cook 100 g of raw chicken breast, it loses about 25–30% of its weight as water. You end up with roughly **70–75 g of cooked chicken**. The protein does not disappear; it is just packed into a smaller, lighter piece.

So:

- **100 g raw breast** ≈ 22–23 g protein
- **That same piece after cooking** ≈ 72 g, still about 22 g protein
- **100 g of cooked breast** ≈ 31 g protein (because it started as about 140 g raw)

If you track your food, pick one method and stick to it. Weighing raw is more accurate. If you buy cooked food (like a meal box), use the cooked numbers.

# How much protein in common chicken dishes?

Homestyle and restaurant dishes vary, but these are good working estimates:

- **One chicken breast (about 170–200 g raw):** about 38–45 g protein
- **One tandoori leg piece (with bone):** about 18–22 g protein
- **Chicken curry, one bowl (about 150 g chicken in gravy):** about 20–25 g protein, but 300–450 kcal depending on oil and coconut
- **Chicken 65, 100 g:** about 18–22 g protein, plus 250–300 kcal because it is deep fried
- **Chicken biryani, one restaurant plate:** about 25–35 g protein, but often 700–1,000 kcal
- **Grilled chicken, 150 g cooked:** about 45–48 g protein, about 250–300 kcal

The protein in fried and gravy dishes is fine. It is the extra oil, coconut and rice that change the calories.

# With bone vs boneless

If you buy chicken "with bone" (curry cut), a good chunk of the weight is bone. Roughly:

- **1 kg curry cut chicken** gives about 650–750 g of edible meat
- So **100 g of curry cut** gives about 15–17 g of protein after you remove the bone

That is why boneless breast is the easiest cut for tracking protein.

# Breast or thigh: which is better?

- **Breast** has the most protein per calorie. Best for fat loss.
- **Thigh** has a little more fat, more flavour and stays juicier. Fine for muscle gain or if you find breast dry.

Both are excellent. If breast feels like "eating cardboard", that is a cooking problem, not a chicken problem. Marinating in curd, lemon, ginger-garlic and spices, then grilling for just long enough, keeps it soft.

# How much chicken do you need a day?

That depends on your total protein target. If you weigh 70 kg and train regularly, about **110 g of protein a day** is a good target (around 1.6 g per kg).

That could look like:

- 150 g cooked chicken at lunch: about 45 g
- 3 eggs at breakfast: about 18 g
- Dal, curd and other food through the day: about 30–40 g
- Another chicken or paneer serving at dinner: about 20–30 g

You do not need to eat only chicken. Mixing sources gives you more nutrients and less boredom. Our [chicken vs eggs vs paneer vs soya comparison](/blog/chicken-eggs-paneer-soya-protein-comparison) breaks down which source wins on price, protein and calories.

# Is eating chicken every day safe?

For most healthy people, yes. Chicken breast is lean, and eating it daily as part of a balanced diet is common among athletes. A few sensible points:

- Choose grilled, boiled, roasted or lightly cooked over deep fried most days.
- Eat vegetables, fibre and some carbs with it; a plate of only chicken is not a balanced meal.
- Cook it fully. Chicken should never be pink inside.
- If you have kidney disease, ask your doctor about your protein limit.

# Frequently asked questions

**How much protein is in 200 g of chicken breast?**
About 62 g if it is 200 g cooked, or about 44–46 g if it is 200 g raw.

**How much protein is in 1 kg of chicken?**
About 220–230 g for raw boneless breast. For curry cut with bone, about 150–170 g.

**Does boiling chicken reduce protein?**
No. Boiling, grilling and baking do not destroy the protein. Some protein can move into the cooking water, so if you boil chicken, use the stock in a soup or rasam.

**Is chicken better than eggs for protein?**
Per 100 g, chicken breast has much more protein than eggs. Eggs are cheaper per serving and very easy to add to any meal. Use both.
