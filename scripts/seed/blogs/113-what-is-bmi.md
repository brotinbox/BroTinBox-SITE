> **Quick answer:** **BMI (body mass index)** is your **weight in kg divided by your height in metres squared**. For **Indian adults**, health guidelines use lower cut-offs than the international ones: **normal is 18.5–22.9**, **overweight is 23–24.9**, and **obese is 25 or above**, because Indians tend to carry more body fat and develop diabetes at a lower BMI. BMI is a quick screening tool, but it **cannot tell fat from muscle**, so check your **waist size** too: **90 cm or more for men and 80 cm or more for women** suggests higher risk.

BMI is the number doctors, insurance forms and fitness apps all use. Here is how to calculate it, what it means for Indians, and where it falls short.

# How to calculate BMI

**BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²**

**Example:** 72 kg, 1.68 m tall
1.68 × 1.68 = 2.82
72 ÷ 2.82 = **BMI 25.5**

# BMI categories

**International (WHO) cut-offs**

- Underweight: below 18.5
- Normal: 18.5–24.9
- Overweight: 25–29.9
- Obese: 30 and above

**Cut-offs commonly used for Indian and other Asian adults**

- Underweight: below 18.5
- Normal: 18.5–22.9
- Overweight: 23–24.9
- Obese: 25 and above

So the example above (BMI 25.5) is "overweight" by international standards, but in the **obese range by Indian cut-offs**.

# Why Indians have lower cut-offs

Research shows that at the same BMI, many South Asians have:

- **More body fat** and less muscle
- **More fat around the belly and organs**
- **Higher risk of type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and heart disease**

That is why Indian guidelines use lower BMI thresholds.

# The limits of BMI

BMI is useful for large groups, but it can mislead individuals:

- **Muscular people** may show as "overweight" even with low body fat
- **"Thin-fat" people** with normal BMI may have high body fat and belly fat
- **It does not show where fat is stored;** belly fat is riskier than fat on the hips
- **Age and sex** affect body composition but not the BMI formula

# Check your waist too

Waist size is a simple, strong indicator of health risk. Measure at the level of your navel, relaxed, after breathing out.

**Indian guidance for higher risk:**

- **Men:** 90 cm (about 35.5 inches) or more
- **Women:** 80 cm (about 31.5 inches) or more

Another simple check: **waist should be less than half your height**. For a 168 cm person, under 84 cm.

# What to do with your BMI

**Under 18.5:** you may need to gain weight; see [weight gain for skinny guys](/blog/weight-gain-for-skinny-guys). Rule out medical causes with a doctor.

**18.5–22.9:** a healthy range. Focus on strength, protein and fitness.

**23–24.9:** a good time to act. Small changes in food and daily steps can prevent further gain.

**25 or above:** aim for gradual weight loss of 5–10% of body weight, which already improves blood sugar and blood pressure. See [how to lose weight in 30 days](/blog/how-to-lose-weight-in-30-days).

**35 or above, or with diabetes or high blood pressure:** work with a doctor on a plan.

# Better ways to track your body

- **Waist measurement** (every week or two)
- **Progress photos**
- **Strength** in the gym
- **Blood tests:** sugar, HbA1c, lipids
- **Body fat estimates** (smart scales are rough, but trends help)

Read [fat loss vs weight loss](/blog/fat-loss-vs-weight-loss) for why the scale is not the whole story.

# Frequently asked questions

**Is BMI accurate?**
It is a rough screening tool. Combine it with waist size and how you feel and perform.

**What is a good BMI for Indian women?**
The same Indian cut-offs apply: 18.5–22.9 is considered normal.

**Can I be healthy with a high BMI?**
If it is from muscle, often yes. If it is from belly fat, risk is higher even if you feel fine.
